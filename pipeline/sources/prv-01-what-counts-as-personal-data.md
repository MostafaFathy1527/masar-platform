# Source note — What counts as personal data, and what to do with it at work

Author's own note, written from public knowledge of data-protection practice. It names no
law, article or regulator, because the lesson is about the habits that every modern
privacy regime expects of an employee, not about any one statute. No client or employer
material, no product names, no real organisations. This is the only permitted input for
generating lesson PRV-01.

**Why this note exists.** Privacy awareness is one of the most commonly commissioned
compliance courses, and it is usually delivered as a slide deck of definitions. This note
is meant to produce a lesson in which the learner decides, case by case, whether a thing
is personal data and what that obliges them to do.

Objective: **PRV-01** — given a piece of information encountered at work, decide whether it
is personal data, whether it is sensitive, and what handling that decision requires.

---

**Personal data** is any information that relates to an identified or identifiable living
person. The test is identifiability, not the obvious label. A name is personal data. So is
an employee number, an email address, a phone number, a photograph, a vehicle plate, a
device identifier, and a home address. So is a combination of ordinarily harmless facts
that, together, point at one person: a job title plus a department plus a start date can
identify someone in a small team as surely as their name.

The question to ask is never "is there a name on it?" It is **"could someone work out who
this is about?"** If the answer is yes, directly or by combining it with something else
reasonably available, it is personal data and the rules apply.

Some personal data is **sensitive**, and the handling bar is higher. Health information,
religion, ethnic origin, political opinions, trade-union membership, biometric and genetic
data, sexual life, and criminal records fall here in most regimes. The reason is not that
these facts are secret; it is that their disclosure can cause harm that cannot be undone.
A sick-leave reason, a dietary requirement that reveals a religion, a photograph that shows
a disability — each is sensitive even when it arrives in an ordinary email.

Three habits cover most of what an employee is expected to do.

**Collect and use only what the task needs.** If a form asks for a date of birth and the
process never uses it, the field is a liability, not a convenience. The same applies to
copying: a spreadsheet exported "just in case" is a second copy of personal data that
nobody is protecting.

**Share on a need-to-know basis, through approved channels.** Forwarding a customer's
details to a colleague who does not need them is a disclosure, even internally. Sending
them over a personal messaging app or a personal email address moves them outside every
control the organisation has, which is why that is the single most common breach in
ordinary office work. The convenient channel is usually the wrong one.

**Keep it only as long as needed, then dispose of it properly.** Personal data in an old
download folder, a departed colleague's mailbox or a shared drive nobody owns is data the
organisation is responsible for and cannot see. Retention has an end date, and deletion
is a task, not an event that happens by itself.

Two situations deserve a specific response. If a person asks what data you hold about them,
asks for it to be corrected, or asks for it to be deleted, that is a **data-subject
request**, and it goes to whoever owns privacy in your organisation on the day it arrives,
not when you get round to it, because most regimes put a clock on the response. If
personal data has gone somewhere it should not have — the wrong recipient, a lost device,
a public link that should have been private — that is a **potential breach**, and the
same rule applies: report it the same day to the person responsible. The organisation's
reporting obligations are measured in hours and days, and the clock usually starts when
any employee becomes aware, not when the privacy officer does.

One thing training usually gets wrong: it presents privacy as a set of prohibitions. The
employee who hides a mis-sent email because they fear the consequences causes more harm
than the one who sent it. A reported breach can be contained; a hidden one is discovered
later, by the person it harmed. The habit to build is not caution for its own sake. It is
to notice when information is about a person, and to treat that as a reason to slow down
and ask.
