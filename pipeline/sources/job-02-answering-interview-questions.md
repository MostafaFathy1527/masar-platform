# Source note — Answering interview questions with examples

Author's own note, written from public knowledge of common interview practice. No employer
material, no named companies, no statistics. The only permitted input for lesson JOB-02.

Objective: **JOB-02** — given a behavioural interview question, build an answer around one real
example that states the situation, the task, the actions taken and the result.

Arabic register: Modern Standard Arabic throughout the Arabic fields.

---

Many interview questions ask about the past to predict the future: "Tell me about a time you
dealt with a difficult customer", "Describe a mistake you made and what you did". The
interviewer is not looking for the perfect story. They want evidence of how you think and act.

**One example, told in order.** A widely used structure has four parts, often remembered as
STAR:
- **Situation** — where and when, in one or two sentences.
- **Task** — what you were responsible for, or what had to be achieved.
- **Action** — what *you* did, step by step. This is the longest part. Say "I", not "we", for
  the parts that were yours.
- **Result** — what happened, and if possible what you learned or would do differently.

**Common ways answers go wrong.** Talking in general ("I always stay calm with customers")
instead of giving one example. Spending most of the answer on the situation and almost none on
the actions. Choosing an example where someone else did the important part. Ending without a
result, which leaves the interviewer to guess.

**Prepare examples, not scripts.** Before the interview, list five or six real situations —
from work, study, volunteering or daily responsibilities — that show different things: solving
a problem, working with others, handling pressure, learning something quickly, making and
fixing a mistake. One good example can answer several questions. Memorising word for word
sounds rehearsed and breaks when the question is slightly different.

**Questions about weaknesses and mistakes** are asking whether you can see your own errors.
A real, modest weakness with what you are doing about it is a strong answer. A disguised
strength ("I work too hard") is usually recognised as avoiding the question.

**It is acceptable to pause** to think, or to ask for a question to be repeated. A short pause
followed by a clear example is better than an immediate vague answer.

**At the end**, interviews usually invite your questions. Asking about the work itself — what
a normal week looks like, how success in the role is judged — shows interest in doing the job,
not only in getting it.
