# Source note — Taking feedback and owning a mistake

Author's own note, written from public knowledge of workplace feedback. No employer material,
no named companies, no statistics. The only permitted input for lesson JOB-06.

Objective: **JOB-06** — given feedback on your work or a mistake you made, respond by
understanding the point, correcting what can be corrected, and saying what will change.

Arabic register: Modern Standard Arabic throughout the Arabic fields.

---

Everyone at work makes mistakes and receives criticism. What colleagues remember is less the
mistake than what the person did next.

**Listen to understand before answering.** When someone gives feedback, the first goal is to know
exactly what they mean. Ask for an example if it is vague: "Can you show me where the report was
unclear?" Explaining or defending yourself before understanding the point usually makes the other
person repeat it more strongly.

**Separate the work from yourself.** Feedback on a task is information about the task. It is not
a judgement of your worth. Thanking someone for specific feedback, even uncomfortable feedback,
makes them more likely to tell you the next time something is wrong.

**Not all feedback is correct.** If you disagree, say so calmly, with your reason, after you have
understood the point. "I see why that section looks incomplete; I left it out because the client
asked for a shorter version. Should I add it back?" Disagreement with a reason is useful;
disagreement as defence is not.

**When you make a mistake, say so early.** Hiding a mistake usually lets it grow. Tell the person
who needs to know, as soon as you know, with four things: what happened, what the effect is, what
you have already done to limit it, and what you will do so it does not happen again.

**Fix what can be fixed.** An apology without action is incomplete. Correct the work, inform
anyone affected, and check whether the same mistake exists elsewhere.

**Change something.** The useful question is not "whose fault was it?" but "what will prevent
this next time?" — a checklist, a second check before sending, a question asked earlier. Saying
what you will change, and then doing it, is what rebuilds trust.

A common mistake is apologising too much, which focuses attention on feelings instead of the
fix. Another is blaming others or circumstances, even when they contributed; take responsibility
for your part first.
