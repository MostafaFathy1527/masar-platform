# Source note — Writing a shift handover

Author's own note, written from public knowledge of shift-based work in operations,
support and service teams. No client or employer material, no product names, no real
organisations. This is the only permitted input for generating lesson OPS-01.

**Why this note exists.** It is the input to the first end-to-end run of the pipeline:
one command takes this note to a draft lesson, through every gate, to a panel of judges,
without a person editing anything in between. The subject was chosen because almost every
organisation that works in shifts has the problem, and almost none teaches it.

Objective: **OPS-01** — given the end of a shift with work still open, write a handover
that the next person can act on without having to contact you.


Arabic register: Modern Standard Arabic throughout the Arabic fields.
---

A handover is the moment work changes hands and context does not. The person leaving
knows why a ticket is half done, which customer is about to call back, and what was
already tried. The person arriving knows none of it. Whatever is not written down at the
end of a shift is, for practical purposes, lost.

The test of a handover is simple: **can the next person act on it without contacting
you?** If they would have to call, message or guess, the handover failed, however long it
was. Length is not the measure. A page of narrative that buries the one urgent item is a
worse handover than three precise lines.

A useful handover answers four questions for every open item, in this order:

1. **What is it?** One line that identifies the item well enough to find it: the ticket,
   the order, the customer, the job.
2. **Where does it stand?** The current state as a fact, not a feeling. "Waiting for the
   supplier to confirm a delivery date" rather than "in progress".
3. **What has already been done?** Especially what was tried and did not work, so the next
   person does not repeat it.
4. **What happens next, and by when?** A specific action with a time attached. "Call the
   customer back before 10:00; they were promised an answer this morning."

**Lead with what is urgent.** Items are ordered by when they need action, not by when
they arrived or how interesting they were. Anything with a deadline inside the next shift
goes first and is marked as such. A handover read from the top should never surprise the
reader at the bottom.

**Separate facts from guesses.** "The payment failed twice" is a fact. "I think the card
is expired" is a guess, and it has to be labelled as one, because the next person will
act on whatever they read as true. An unlabelled guess becomes the next shift's fact.

**Commitments travel with the work.** If a customer, a colleague or a manager was promised
something — a call back, a refund, an update by a time — that promise is now the next
person's, and the handover must say so in words: who was promised what, and by when.
A broken promise is not excused by a shift change; to the person who was promised, the
organisation made it, not the individual.

**Write it where the work lives.** A handover on a scrap of paper, a private message or
a personal note cannot be found by whoever covers the shift after next. Write it in the
shared record — the ticket, the log, the team channel the team agreed on — so it survives
the person who wrote it.

**Common failures**, each of which the next person pays for:

- "All good, nothing major" when there is one open item with a deadline.
- Status words that carry no information: "ongoing", "pending", "being looked at".
- Naming a problem without saying what was already tried.
- Leaving out a promise because it felt informal at the time it was made.
- Writing the handover in the last two minutes, from memory, instead of during the shift.

A handover is a short piece of writing with a hard reader: someone tired, starting cold,
who will act on exactly what is written. Write for that reader.
