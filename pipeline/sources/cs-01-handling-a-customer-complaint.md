# Source note — Handling a customer complaint

Author's own note, written from public knowledge of frontline service work. No client or
employer material, no product names, no real organisations. This is the only permitted
input for generating lesson CS-01.

**Why this note exists.** It is the second pipeline run outside the demo course's domain,
and the first in a subject that corporate training buyers actually commission at volume:
customer service. SEC-01 showed the pipeline is not locked to medical insurance. This note
is meant to produce a lesson a training manager could put in front of a service team on
Monday.

Objective: **CS-01** — given a customer complaint, de-escalate it, establish the facts, and
close it with an agreed next step that the customer can hold you to.

---

A complaint is not an attack and it is not a request for an apology. It is a customer
telling you that the gap between what they expected and what they got has become worth
their time to report. Most customers who are let down say nothing and leave. The one who
complains is giving you the chance the others did not.

The first minute decides the next twenty. A customer who opens angry is carrying two
problems: the thing that went wrong, and the feeling that nobody has listened. The second
has to be dealt with first, because a person who does not feel heard cannot hear anything
you say. **Acknowledgement** is the move: name what happened in their words, and say that
you understand why it matters, before you say anything about causes or fixes. "You were
charged twice and nobody called you back, and you have spent an hour on this. I can see why
you are angry." That is not agreeing that your organisation was wrong; it is agreeing that
the customer's experience was bad, which is a fact they own.

What acknowledgement is not: an apology for something you have not yet established, a
defence of a policy, or a promise. "I'm sorry you feel that way" is the sentence that makes
complaints worse, because it apologises for the feeling rather than the event, and the
customer hears exactly that.

Only after the temperature drops do you **establish the facts**, and the order is: what
happened, when, what the customer has already done about it, and what they now want. The
last question is the one most agents skip and the one that saves the most time. People
complaining about a late delivery sometimes want a refund, sometimes want the item today,
and sometimes only want to be told the truth about when it will arrive. Ask, do not guess.
A fix the customer did not ask for is a second complaint.

Facts come from systems, not from either party's memory. Check the record while the
customer is with you and say what you are doing: "I am looking at the order now." Silence
on a call or in a chat reads as abandonment. If the record and the customer disagree, say
what the record shows without calling the customer wrong, and treat the disagreement as
something to investigate rather than a verdict.

**Ownership** means the complaint has one name on it until it is closed, and that name is
yours. Transferring a customer is sometimes necessary; transferring them without a warm
handover, so they explain everything again, is the second most common cause of an
escalation after not being listened to. If you must pass it on, pass the facts with it,
and tell the customer who now holds it and when they will hear back.

The close is an **agreed next step**: a specific action, a specific time, and a specific
way the customer will know it happened. "I will raise the refund now; it reaches your card
within five working days; you will get a confirmation by email today." Vague closes
("someone will look into it") are where complaints become repeat complaints, because the
customer has nothing to hold the organisation to, and so calls back to create it.

Three things that should not happen, however the conversation goes. Do not promise what
you cannot personally make true; promise what you can do and the time by which you will
report back. Do not argue the policy; explain it once, then move to what can be done
within it. Do not take the anger personally, and do not match it; the customer is angry at
a situation and you are the first person who could change it.

Finally, a complaint that is closed well is worth recording honestly, including what went
wrong on the organisation's side. A complaint log that only records "resolved" teaches
nothing. The pattern across fifty complaints is where the next fix lives, and the agent on
the call is the only person positioned to capture it.
